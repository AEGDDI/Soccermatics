FC Barcelona Tactical Evolution (2004–2021) — Part 2: Passing Networks

A data-driven exploration of how player roles and possession structure evolved across Barcelona’s La Liga seasons

Victoria Friss de Kereki
6 min read
·
May 11, 2026

--

In Part 1, we explored how FC Barcelona’s passing structure evolved through pass clustering, revealing how spatial patterns changed across seasons.

But structure alone does not tell the full story.

Understanding how a team plays also requires analysing how control is distributed within that structure — how possession is allocated across players, and how that distribution evolves over time.

Passing networks provide this perspective. If clustering explains what happens on the pitch, networks explain who makes it happen.

🔗 Python code & notebook: available on my GitHub.

From structure to control

A passing network represents players as nodes and passes as connections between them. Instead of focusing on the types of passes, it captures how possession flows through the team.

This shift reframes the analysis. Rather than describing how Barcelona use space, we begin to understand how the team organises control within that space.

Across seasons, this reveals whether possession is shared collectively or channelled through specific players — and how that balance changes over time.

How to read a passing network

We begin with a single example to establish how passing networks should be interpreted.

In this representation:

  • Each node represents a role within the team, not a fixed player
  • Node location reflects the average position of that role
  • Edges represent completed passes between roles
  • Thicker and more opaque links indicate more frequent exchanges

Rather than fixing players to positions, roles emerge from behaviour: players are grouped based on where they operate on the pitch. The network is not a formation diagram — it is a behavioural representation of how roles emerge and interact.

Substitutions are naturally incorporated into this framework. Players who occupy the same role — whether across different matches or within the same match — are mapped to the same node. This allows the network to reflect role continuity rather than individual presence.

In the 2005/06 example above, Ronaldinho (left), Samuel Eto’o (centre), and Lionel Messi (right) appear almost aligned across a similar line, reflecting how the attacking unit functions collectively rather than as isolated roles.

This provides a snapshot of how possession is organised: which roles connect play, how the ball circulates, and how involvement is distributed.

Barcelona passing networks across seasons

We can now extend this analysis across the full timeline.

Across seasons, the overall structure of Barcelona’s passing networks remains remarkably consistent. A clear defensive base is always visible, typically built around two centre-backs, with Carles Puyol frequently anchoring the back line in the earlier years.

While this structure is stable, subtle variations emerge, for example:

  • In some seasons (e.g. 2011/12), the defensive line appears more compact
  • In others (e.g. 2015/16), it becomes more stretched

These differences reflect small but meaningful changes in build-up shape.

Central role in possession

Beyond the defensive line, a clear central hub consistently appears. From around 2006/07 to 2013/14, this role is largely occupied by Xavi Hernández, who acts as the primary connector between phases of play, as seen in the 2010/11 passing network presented below.

In this example, Xavi sits at the centre of the network, deeply connected to those around him — far more than any other player. His links extend across the entire structure: combining constantly with Sergio Busquets and Andrés Iniesta in midfield, connecting with Lionel Messi in advanced areas, and linking wide with Dani Alves. His influence even reaches further back into the defensive line, with regular connections to players such as Eric Abidal and Gerard Piqué.

This pattern reflects his role as the primary organiser of possession — constantly receiving, recycling, and redistributing the ball to maintain control and tempo. In later seasons, this responsibility shifts more frequently towards Andrés Iniesta.

This highlights a key idea: the structure remains stable, but who controls it changes over time.

Attack structured around Messi

In attacking areas, the network becomes increasingly shaped around Lionel Messi. Advanced connections consistently converge towards him, possession is funnelled into his zones, and he acts as the main reference point in attack.

In this example, Messi occupies a central position within the attacking structure, with a dense web of connections linking him closely to midfield and to the other forward roles. Passes into the final third are frequently routed through him, and combinations in advanced areas tend to revolve around his involvement.

Unlike a traditional forward, Messi is not just an end point but a connector. He drops deeper to combine with midfield — particularly Xavi and Andrés Iniesta — while Dani Alves provides width and forward support. From these deeper positions, Messi progresses play and reappears in attacking zones, often receiving between the lines and accelerating the attack.

The result is an attacking structure organised not simply around space, but around a player capable of both orchestrating and finishing actions. In effect, Barcelona play through Messi.

From centralised attack to shared responsibility

This attacking dynamic began to evolve with the arrivals of Neymar in 2013 and Luis Suárez in 2014.

With multiple elite attacking profiles, the structure in the final third becomes more distributed. Possession is no longer channelled as consistently through a single focal point, and attacking responsibility is shared more evenly across the forward line. The network reflects this shift, with connections spreading more broadly between roles rather than converging predominantly towards one player.

In this example, the attacking network is less concentrated around a single node. Connections in advanced areas are shared more evenly between roles, reflecting a front line in which multiple players contribute to progression and chance creation.

However, Lionel Messi remains central to the system. Even within a more balanced attacking structure, he continues to act as a key reference point in advanced areas — linking play, receiving in dangerous spaces, and shaping the final phase of possession.

The result is not a complete departure from earlier patterns, but a rebalancing: from a highly centralised attacking structure to one that distributes responsibility more widely, while still orbiting around Messi’s influence.

What passing networks reveal

Across seasons, Barcelona’s passing networks highlight two consistent themes: a stable positional structure and a changing distribution of involvement.

While the overall shape of the team remains largely consistent, the way possession is organised within that structure evolves. Certain players become more central to circulation, shaping the rhythm, direction, and progression of play. Control is not fixed — it shifts depending on personnel and tactical context.

Passing networks make this visible. They move the analysis beyond where the team plays, towards understanding who drives how it plays.
