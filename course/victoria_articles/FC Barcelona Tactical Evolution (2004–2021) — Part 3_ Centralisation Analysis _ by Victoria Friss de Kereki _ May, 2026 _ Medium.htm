FC Barcelona Tactical Evolution (2004–2021) — Part 3: Centralisation Analysis

A data-driven exploration of how control of possession evolved across Barcelona’s La Liga seasons

Victoria Friss de Kereki
8 min read
·
May 14, 2026

--

In Part 2, we used passing networks to understand how possession flowed through FC Barcelona. We saw that while the team’s positional structure remained remarkably stable, the distribution of involvement within that structure changed over time. But visual analysis has limits.

Passing networks make patterns visible — they show who is more connected, who acts as a hub, and how possession is organised. However, they do not tell us how much more central one player is compared to the rest, or how that balance shifts across seasons in a measurable way.

To move beyond interpretation, this article introduces a formal measure of network centralisation, allowing us to track how control of possession is distributed — and how it evolves over time.

Measuring centralisation

Centralisation captures how evenly passing involvement is distributed across players.

  • When possession is shared evenly → low centralisation
  • When a few players dominate → high centralisation

The approach used here follows the framework popularised in football analytics by David Sumpter in Soccermatics, building on earlier work by Thomas Grund and the broader literature on social network analysis.

The idea is to compare each player’s involvement in passing to that of the most involved player in the team.

For each player, we compute the difference between passes they received and those of the most involved player. These differences are then summed across all players and normalised by the total number of passes in the team.

Interpreting the metric

The centralisation index ranges between 0 and 1:

  • 0 → fully distributed network
  • 1 → highly centralised network

In practical terms, lower values indicate a more even distribution of passing involvement across players, while higher values indicate concentration around a small number of key individuals.

At the extremes, the interpretation becomes clearer. A value of 0 would correspond to a perfectly balanced system in which every player receives exactly the same number of passes per 90. While this is theoretically possible, it is highly unlikely in real matches given positional roles and tactical structure.

At the other end, a value of 1 would represent a fully centralised network in which a single player receives all passes, and all other players receive none. This scenario is practically impossible in football, as it would imply that possession is entirely channelled through one individual without any redistribution.

Because the metric is normalised by total passes, it is directly comparable across seasons, allowing changes in control to be tracked over time regardless of differences in match volume or tempo. The measure is based exclusively on completed passes, ensuring that it reflects how possession is actually distributed on the pitch, rather than merely the volume of passing attempts.

Centralisation should not be interpreted purely as a descriptive statistic. Thomas Grund shows that more evenly distributed passing structures are often associated with stronger team performance, while David Sumpter highlights that decentralised teams tend to be less predictable and harder to defend against.

However, the relationship is not deterministic. Even relatively small changes in centralisation can reflect meaningful tactical differences in how teams organise control and responsibility.

Methodological note: constructing the centralisation measure

The worked example above illustrates how centralisation is computed within a single match. A few additional methodological choices are important for interpreting the results across matches and seasons.

The metric is calculated at match level using completed passes, with each player’s involvement expressed on a per-90 basis. This ensures that differences in playing time — due to substitutions or rotation — do not distort the distribution of control.

To maintain a consistent network structure, only players exceeding a minimum playing time threshold are included. This avoids brief appearances artificially inflating or compressing centralisation values.

Goalkeepers are excluded from the calculation, as their passing role is structurally different from that of outfield players and would otherwise dampen variation in the network.

Centralisation is computed separately for each match and then aggregated at season level. This ensures that the final measure reflects consistent patterns of possession distribution, rather than being driven by individual matches or squad selection effects.

Centralisation over time

Before examining how Barcelona evolves, it is important to first understand what the centralisation metric looks like in a real competitive context. Without this reference, the values are difficult to interpret in isolation. To anchor the measure, we begin by looking at a full La Liga 2015/16 season.

This league-wide distribution shows a clear spread in how teams organise possession. Centralisation values vary meaningfully across clubs: some teams operate around more distributed passing structures, while others concentrate involvement more strongly within a smaller number of players.

Importantly, Barcelona sits on the lower end of this distribution, with an average centralisation of approximately 0.0418 that season, compared to league values that typically range closer to 0.05–0.07. This places them among the more evenly distributed possession systems in the league, even though all teams still operate within a relatively constrained tactical range.

This context is essential: centralisation is not an absolute scale of “good” or “bad”, but a relative measure of how control is organised within a team’s passing structure.

With this reference in place, we can now interpret Barcelona’s evolution over time.

The table below shows Barcelona’s centralisation index across seasons, allowing changes in the distribution of control to be tracked over time.

Rather than following a single linear trend, Barcelona’s centralisation evolves through a series of gradual adjustments, reflecting changes in how control is distributed within a largely stable structure.

In the early seasons (2004–2008), centralisation remains moderate, averaging between 0.045 and 0.055. Passing involvement is relatively well distributed, with no single dominant hub consistently emerging. Responsibility is shared across multiple players, including figures such as Deco and Ronaldinho, rather than being channelled through a fixed organising point.

From 2008 to 2012, centralisation increases and stabilises at a higher level, reaching 0.0612 at its peak. This represents the most concentrated phase in terms of control distribution. During this period, Xavi becomes the clear organisational hub of the team, consistently acting as the primary reference point in possession. The structure remains stable, but influence becomes more clearly anchored around his role in midfield circulation.

From 2013 onwards, this concentration begins to ease. Centralisation declines, reaching 0.0444 in 2014/15 and 0.0418 in 2015/16 — among the lowest values observed across the full period. This shift reflects a redistribution of control, with influence spreading more evenly across players such as Iniesta and Neymar, rather than being dominated by a single midfield hub.

In the later seasons (2016–2021), centralisation stabilises within a relatively narrow range. The system settles into a consistent pattern: possession remains structured, but not heavily concentrated. During this phase, the role of the hub becomes more fluid. Messi increasingly acts as a focal point in advanced areas, while players such as Busquets organise deeper circulation. Rather than a single fixed centre, control is shared across clearly defined roles.

When viewed alongside the passing networks presented in Part 2, this evolution becomes more coherent. The overall positional structure remains remarkably stable across seasons, but the identity of the main hub — and how strongly influence is concentrated around it — changes over time. Centralisation captures this shift quantitatively: not a transformation of shape, but a redistribution of influence within it.

Linking structure and control

Across the three parts of this analysis, we build a layered understanding of the same system:

  • Part 1 (Clustering): how passing patterns are structured spatially
  • Part 2 (Networks): how players are connected within those patterns
  • Part 3 (Centralisation): how control is distributed across those connections

Each layer moves the analysis from spatial organisation, to relational structure, to concentration of influence. Taken together, these perspectives show that structure and control are not the same thing. A team can maintain a consistent positional framework while significantly altering how responsibility is distributed within it.

Final insight: control within structure

Across this period, Barcelona do not radically change how they are positioned on the pitch. What changes is how control is distributed within that structure.

At times, possession is more evenly shared across the team. At others, it is more clearly organised around a dominant hub — whether that is Xavi in midfield, or later Messi and Busquets in different phases of play.

These shifts are subtle, but measurable. Centralisation shows that even within a stable positional system, the balance of influence can move significantly.

When combined with the passing networks from Part 2, a clearer picture emerges: Barcelona’s evolution is not about structural reinvention, but about reassigning responsibility within the same framework.

In that sense, tactical identity is not replaced over time — it is continuously redistributed.
