Who Faces the Hardest Passes in Women’s Football?

A data-driven analysis using Expected Pass (xP) to measure difficulty, execution, and positional demands across Women’s Euros and World Cups

Victoria Friss de Kereki
10 min read
·
Feb 19, 2026

--

In my previous article, Expected Pass (xP) in Women’s Football: How to Calibrate Pass Predictions for Accuracy, I showed how to calibrate a model so that xP, the probability a pass is successfully completed, matches what actually happens on the pitch. Calibration ensures predicted probabilities reflect real match outcomes, allowing passes to be compared fairly across players and positions.

With a reliable xP model, we can move from probabilities to insight. Which positions face the most difficult passing situations in elite international tournaments? And when difficulty rises, which players still complete more passes than the model predicts? By analysing xP across positions and individuals, we can evaluate passing quality in context rather than relying on raw completion rates alone.

This article first identifies which players outperform passing expectations, then examines how passing difficulty varies by position, and finally explores individual pass maps to show how these challenges emerge in real match situations.

🔗 Full Python code available in my GitHub.

A Brief Recap: What xP Is and How the Model Works

Expected Pass (xP) estimates the probability that a pass will be completed, accounting for context such as location, pressure, distance, and angle. Unlike raw completion rates, it reflects both difficulty and outcome. For example, a short unpressured pass across the defensive line may have an xP above 0.8, while a long aerial ball into a contested attacking area might fall below 0.3. These probabilities represent the average success rate of similar passes observed across many matches.

In my earlier work, I developed the xP model I’ll be using in this article, using event data from StatsBomb and key contextual pass features. I then calibrated the model so predicted probabilities align with real match outcomes for Women’s Euros and Women’s World Cup. With calibrated xP, we can compare passes across positions and evaluate player execution relative to pass difficulty with confidence.

Passing Quality: Measuring Execution Per Player

The first step in evaluating passing performance is assessing how well players execute passes relative to their difficulty. Scatterplots comparing average expected pass completion (xP) with actual completion relative to expectation — calculated as completed passes minus expected completions, divided by total passes — highlight players who consistently exceed or underperform expectations. Each point represents a player and is colour-coded by position. To reduce small-sample noise, only players with at least 50 passes are included.

The horizontal axis reflects average pass difficulty, with lower xP values indicating more challenging passes. The vertical axis shows execution relative to expectation: players above the zero line complete more passes than the model predicts, while those below complete fewer.

Players in the top-right combine difficult passing environments with above-expected execution, representing the strongest performers. Bottom-right players attempt difficult passes but complete fewer than expected. Top-left players complete many passes, but primarily easier ones. Bottom- left players attempt easier passes and have a below average completion rate.

FIFA Women’s World Cup

The following scatterplot shows passing for FIFA Women’s World Cups 2019 & 2023.

Looking at the two tournaments, two players stand out for their performance relative to expectation: Sarina Bolden and Marta Cox.

Bolden, a forward for the Philippines, scored her country’s first-ever Women’s World Cup goal in 2023, helping secure their first tournament victory. Cox achieved a similar milestone for Panama, scoring her nation’s first goal at the same competition. Beyond these historic moments, both players demonstrate exceptional passing performance relative to difficulty, consistently completing more passes than the model predicts. Their ability to connect play under pressure highlights strong vision and technical execution in demanding match contexts.

The scatterplot also reveals a clear role-based pattern. Goalkeepers cluster among players with the lowest average xP, reflecting the inherent difficulty of long distributions from deep positions. Their unique passing profile contrasts with outfield players, who operate in comparatively less risky passing contexts.

UEFA Women’s Euro

The following scatterplot shows passing for UEFA Women’s Euros 2022 & 2025.

A similar pattern as seen in the World Cup scatterplot emerges in the Euros one. Two forwards stand out for sustained performance above expectation: Elena Dhont and Agla María Albertsdóttir. Both operate above the tournament median in pass difficulty while maintaining elite completion relative to expectation, demonstrating composure and technical precision in demanding situations.

One player occupies an especially notable position in the top-right of the distribution: Sveindís Jane Jónsdóttir. She combines extremely high pass difficulty with strong execution. This raises an interesting tactical question: does she consistently attempt harder passes because safer options are limited, or is it because her technical ability allows her to pursue more aggressive and potentially more valuable passing choices?

Consistent Themes Across Tournaments

Across competitions, consistent themes emerge: some players reliably exceed expectations in high-difficulty passing environments, while others struggle to meet predicted outcomes. Positional structure is also evident — goalkeepers consistently attempt the most difficult passes, while outfield players generally operate in less risky passing contexts. These player-level insights establish who performs above expectation and provide a foundation for exploring how positional roles influence passing difficulty itself.

Passing Difficulty: Goalkeepers vs Outfield Players

With individual passing quality established, the next step is to examine how passing difficulty differs systematically between goalkeepers and outfield players — a contrast already suggested by the scatterplots.

To begin, we compare average xP per pass by position using boxplots. Lower average xP indicates more difficult passing situations.

A clear pattern emerges: goalkeepers consistently attempt the most difficult passes, reflected in the lowest median xP values and boxes. Midfielders, in contrast, tend to make relatively easier passes, with a higher median xP and a distribution shifted upward, reflecting shorter and more controlled circulation.

Several structural features explain this positional gap.

Length

One factor is pass length, plotted in the following density plot.

As seen above, goalkeepers frequently play long distributions, often concentrated around 50 units on the standardised 120-unit pitch. Longer passes are inherently more difficult, a relationship already captured in the xP model.

Progression

Another factor is how much do passes advance the ball upfield, shown in the density plot that follows.

Goalkeeper passes typically advance the ball further upfield, while outfield players more often recycle possession through lateral or backward movement. Greater progression increases risk and reduces expected completion probability.

Height

Pass height is also an important contributor to difficulty. The distribution of high passes for goalkeepers and outfield players is shown in the plots below.

Goalkeepers also attempt a higher proportion of aerial passes, which are generally harder to control and therefore associated with lower completion likelihood.

Integrating Pass Characteristics

To consolidate these patterns, we summarise average and median pass length, progression, and high pass (binary variable) across positions. Together, these characteristics define goalkeeper distributions as longer, more progressive, and more frequently aerial — all factors that systematically increase difficulty and lower expected completion relative to outfield players.

Importantly, this does not mean every goalkeeper pass is harder than every outfield pass. Context still matters: pitch location, pressure, and tactical situation can produce easier or harder distributions for any player. The analysis instead identifies the structural tendencies that most consistently shape positional differences in passing difficulty.

Examining Individual Passes: Goalkeepers and Outfield Players

To see how passing difficulty plays out in practice, we move from averages to individual pass maps. These visualisations show how position, distance, progression, and trajectory create unique challenges for goalkeepers and outfield players, and how each adapts to those demands.

Goalkeepers: Long Distributions and Territorial Gain

Goalkeepers face a distinct type of difficulty: long, progressive passes that shift the ball quickly from defence to attack. These passes often travel significant distances, sometimes under pressure, and frequently target contested areas, which lowers their xP.

Cata Coll’s passes during the 2023 World Cup span large areas of the pitch, many moving well beyond the defensive third. Darker arrows, indicating lower xP, highlight riskier long balls. While not all are successful, the pattern shows how goalkeepers trade completion probability for territorial advantage, using distribution as both a defensive reset and an attacking tool.

Hannah Hampton’s Euros 2025 pass map emphasises even longer ranges, with several very low-xP attempts. These distributions illustrate the tactical trade-off of deep-field passes: they are harder to complete but can rapidly advance play into opposition territory.

Takeaway: Goalkeepers face difficulty primarily through distance, height, and progression, making long distributions both high-risk and high-reward.

Outfield Players: Context, Pressure, and Decision-Making

Outfield players encounter difficulty differently. Passes are generally shorter but occur in congested spaces and often require split-second decisions. Difficulty is less about distance and more about pressure, limited options, and tactical intent.

Olga Carmona’s passes in the 2025 Euros reflect her mixed defensive left-back and winger role, with many short, safe connections along the flank. Darker arrows appear mostly in advanced positions where space is tighter, highlighting moments when attacking responsibility increases passing difficulty.

Ella Toone’s passes in the 2022 and 2025 Euros reflect her attacking midfielder role, covering most areas of the pitch with a mix of passing types. Most passes are short or backward, but darker arrows appear in advanced positions or longer balls into the opposition half, highlighting moments when greater distance or pressure increases passing difficulty.

Alessia Russo’s 2023 World Cup map shows she operates mostly in attacking areas, with most passes short and controlled. Darker arrows cluster near the penalty area, showing where timing and precision become critical. Even short passes can carry great difficulty under pressure.

Takeaway: Outfield difficulty emerges from spatial pressure, tactical choices, and decision-making speed, rather than sheer distance. Passes under pressure can be as challenging as long goalkeeping distributions.

What These Pass Maps Reveal

Passing difficulty is multifaceted and shaped by position-specific demands:

  • Goalkeepers face risk through long, high, and progressive distributions.
  • Outfield players navigate tight spaces, high-pressure zones, and split-second decision-making.

Within these roles, individual skill becomes apparent. Coll and Hampton show calm and precision in managing long, challenging distributions, while Carmona and Russo excel in congested areas, executing difficult passes under tactical and spatial pressure.

Together, these examples illustrate that high passing quality relies on a blend of technical execution and intelligent decision-making. Whether launching the ball from deep territory or threading it through crowded attacking areas, players must adapt to the unique challenges of their role — each type of pass representing a different form of pressure and skill.

Conclusion: Seeing the Game Through xP

Expected Pass (xP) does more than measure completion — it quantifies challenge. By combining probability with context, xP allows us to see where passing is hardest, which players thrive under pressure, and how positional demands shape the flow of the game.

Across Women’s Euros and World Cups, a clear story emerges:

  • Goalkeepers shoulder the burden of long, progressive distributions, turning risk into a tactical weapon.
  • Outfield players navigate congested spaces and high-pressure scenarios, where split-second decisions and technical precision define success.
  • Exceptional performers — from Bolden and Cox to Jónsdóttir and Dhont — show that exceptional passing is as much about vision and timing as it is about execution.

xP reframes how we understand passing. It highlights not just what happens on the ball, but the why behind it — revealing the subtle interplay of risk, skill, and decision-making that shapes elite women’s football.

In short, the hardest passes don’t just test technique — they illuminate vision, composure, and intelligence. And through this lens, we can truly appreciate the players who turn pressure into performance.

The hardest passes reveal more than technique — they reveal the vision and courage that define the game.
